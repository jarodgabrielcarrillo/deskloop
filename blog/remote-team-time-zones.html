Best Time Zones for Remote Team Standups (And How to Find Them)

📅 May 2026 ⏰ 7 min read 💬 Remote teams & scheduling

Scheduling a daily standup for a team spread across multiple time zones is one of those problems that sounds simple until you try to actually solve it. New York and London have a 5-hour gap that’s manageable. Add Singapore and you’re spanning 13 hours. Add San Francisco and you’ve run out of business hours entirely.

There is no perfect solution when a team is genuinely global. But there are better and worse approaches, and understanding the tradeoffs makes the decision clearer.

The most common time zone combinations and their overlap windows

For teams with members in two or three time zones, there’s usually a workable overlap window during standard business hours. Here are the most common combinations:

US East Coast + Western Europe (New York + London)

This is one of the more manageable combinations. With a 5-hour gap (6 during US daylight saving time), there’s solid overlap from 2pm–5pm London / 9am–12pm New York. A 9am Eastern standup works well for both sides — it’s mid-afternoon in London but still a reasonable working hour.

US West Coast + Eastern Europe (San Francisco + Warsaw)

The gap here is 9–10 hours, which makes overlap tight. The only workable window is roughly 4pm–6pm Warsaw / 7am–9am San Francisco. This means either early mornings in California or late afternoons in Poland. Neither is comfortable long-term.

Europe + Asia Pacific (London + Singapore)

An 8-hour gap with limited overlap. The viable window is approximately 9am–11am Singapore / 1am–3am London — which only works asynchronously. For a synchronous standup, someone will always be outside normal hours. The least bad option is 4pm–6pm Singapore / 8am–10am London, which requires Singapore to take a late-day call.

Full global spread (New York + London + Singapore)

With three continents, there is no window that falls within business hours for all three. You have two realistic options: find the window that’s least bad for the majority and compensate the outlier, or rotate the meeting time so the burden is shared.

Rule of thumb: Two time zones almost always have overlap. Three is manageable. Four or more almost always requires asynchronous communication to carry the load.

How Daylight Saving Time complicates everything

Most countries that observe Daylight Saving Time don’t change their clocks on the same date. The US and Europe typically switch several weeks apart. Singapore, Japan, India, and most of Asia don’t observe DST at all.

This means the gap between two cities can shift by an hour or two at certain times of year, moving a meeting that fell within business hours to outside them. A 9am New York / 2pm London standup becomes a 9am New York / 1pm London standup when the US switches before the UK — which is fine — but can become a 9am New York / 3pm London meeting when the UK switches first. The variations are small but they matter when the overlap window is already tight.

Always check current, DST-adjusted offsets when scheduling recurring meetings, not just the standard UTC offset.

Strategies for teams with no good overlap

When the math doesn’t work, the answer isn’t to force a meeting at an unreasonable hour every day. These approaches reduce the synchronous meeting burden:

  • Async standups. Team members post a short text or voice note at the start of their day answering the standard standup questions (what I did yesterday, what I’m doing today, any blockers). Tools like Loom, Slack, or even a shared document handle this well.
  • Rotating meeting times. Instead of one time zone always taking the early or late call, rotate the meeting time weekly or monthly. This distributes the inconvenience fairly.
  • Reduce meeting frequency. If daily standups are genuinely impractical across time zones, consider whether they need to be daily. Weekly synchronous meetings supplemented by async updates can work well for many teams.
  • Designate overlap hours. Agree on a 2–3 hour window each day where everyone is expected to be available for synchronous communication, and schedule all meetings within it. Outside that window, async is the default.

The fairness question

When there’s no good overlap, someone is always making a sacrifice. The common default — setting the meeting time to work for the largest office or the headquarters time zone — consistently burdens the same people.

Over time, this creates a real equity problem. Team members who are always taking 7am or 7pm calls are experiencing a different employment condition than those who always meet during normal hours. This affects wellbeing, retention, and perceived inclusion in the team.

The most defensible approach is to rotate the burden explicitly, acknowledge it openly, and compensate people who regularly take calls outside normal hours with flexibility in other areas — later starts, earlier finishes, or additional autonomy over their schedule.

Good remote teams treat time zone fairness as a real management problem, not a scheduling inconvenience.
