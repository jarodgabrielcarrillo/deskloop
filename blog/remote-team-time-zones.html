Best Time Zones for
Remote Team Standups

When a team spans New York, London, and Singapore, there’s no good standup time. But there are better and worse ways to handle it.

Scheduling a daily standup across time zones sounds simple. It is not. New York and London have a 5-hour gap. Add Singapore and you span 13 hours. Add San Francisco and there are no business hours that work for everyone. There is no perfect solution when a team is genuinely global, but there are better and worse ways to handle it.

5h
Gap between New York and London. One of the most manageable cross-Atlantic time zone combinations.
13h
Gap between New York and Singapore. Essentially no overlap during standard business hours.

The most common time zone combinations and their overlap windows

For teams in two or three time zones, a workable overlap window during standard business hours usually exists. The most common combinations:

US East Coast + Western Europe (New York + London)

With a 5-hour gap (6 during US daylight saving), there is solid overlap from 2pm to 5pm London / 9am to 12pm New York. A 9am Eastern standup works well for both sides. This is one of the more sustainable cross-Atlantic arrangements.

US West Coast + Eastern Europe (San Francisco + Warsaw)

The 9 to 10 hour gap makes overlap tight. The only window is roughly 4pm to 6pm Warsaw / 7am to 9am San Francisco. That means early mornings in California or late afternoons in Poland. Neither is comfortable long-term, which makes rotating the burden important.

Europe + Asia Pacific (London + Singapore)

An 8-hour gap with limited overlap. The viable window is approximately 9am to 11am Singapore / 1am to 3am London — which only works asynchronously for most teams. For synchronous standups, someone is always outside normal hours.

Fully global (New York + London + Singapore)

With a 13-hour span, no time works during business hours for everyone. The only solution is asynchronous communication, with synchronous meetings reserved for high-value occasions and rotated across time zones.

How Daylight Saving Time complicates everything

DST does not switch everywhere on the same date. The US and Europe switch in different weeks. Most of Asia and Africa do not observe it at all. This creates a period each spring and autumn where the gap between two locations shifts by an hour temporarily.

A 9am New York / 2pm London standup becomes a 9am New York / 3pm London meeting when clocks switch on different dates. Small shift. Significant when the overlap window is already tight.

DST Warning

Always check current, DST-adjusted offsets when scheduling recurring meetings, not just the standard UTC offset. Set calendar events in local time zones, not fixed UTC, so they adjust automatically when clocks change.

Strategies for teams with no good overlap

When the math doesn’t work, forcing a synchronous meeting at an unreasonable hour every day isn’t the answer. These approaches reduce the burden:

  • Async standups. Each person posts a short update at the start of their day: yesterday, today, blockers. Loom, Slack, or a shared doc all work. No scheduling required.
  • Rotating meeting times. Rotate the inconvenience. Weekly or monthly rotation distributes the burden fairly instead of assigning it permanently to one region.
  • Reduce meeting frequency. If daily standups are impractical across your time zones, they probably should not be daily. Weekly synchronous meetings plus async updates often produce the same result.
  • Designate overlap hours. Agree on a 2 to 3 hour window where everyone is reachable. Schedule all meetings inside it. Outside that window, async is the default.

The fairness question

When no good overlap exists, someone always makes a sacrifice. The default — setting meeting times to suit headquarters — consistently burdens the same people.

Over time, this creates a real equity problem. Team members who always take 7am or 7pm calls experience a materially different working condition than those who always meet during normal hours. It affects wellbeing, retention, and perceived inclusion.

Fairness Principle

The most defensible approach is to rotate the burden explicitly, acknowledge it openly, and compensate people who regularly take calls outside normal hours with flexibility elsewhere — later starts, earlier finishes, or additional schedule autonomy.

Practical recommendations by team size

The right approach depends on how spread your team is:

  • Two time zones, under 8 hours apart: Find a standing time that works for both. Rotate if one side always takes the worse slot.
  • Two time zones, 8 to 12 hours apart: Async for daily standups. One synchronous weekly meeting at a rotating time.
  • Three or more time zones: Designate overlap hours. Keep synchronous meetings rare and high-value. Treat shared real-time availability as the scarce resource it is.
  • Fully global (12+ hour span): Daily synchronous standups are not realistic. Design around async as the default. Reserve synchronous time for relationship-building and complex decisions.
Takeaway

Map your actual overlap window first. Then design your meeting culture around what the math allows. What headquarters finds convenient is not a good enough reason to put someone on a 7am call every week.
