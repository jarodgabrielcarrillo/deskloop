How to Write a Freelance Invoice
That Gets Paid Fast

A slow-paying client is sometimes a bad client. But often they are responding to an invoice that made it easy to delay. The right structure, clear terms, and a professional format removes most of the friction that causes late payments.

Required fields on every invoice

An invoice missing key information gives a client a legitimate reason to delay — they need clarification before they can process payment. Every invoice you send should include:

  • Your full legal name or business name and contact details — email and phone at minimum. The client’s accounts team needs to know who to pay and how to reach you if there is a question.
  • A unique invoice number. Sequential numbering (INV-001, INV-002) makes it easy for both parties to reference specific invoices in follow-up communications.
  • Issue date and due date. These two dates define the payment window. Without a due date, any stated term has no anchor point.
  • An itemised list of services. Each line item should include a description, quantity or hours, rate, and line total. Vague descriptions like “design work” invite disputes. “Homepage redesign — 8 hours at $75/hr” does not.
  • Payment instructions. Bank transfer details, PayPal address, or whichever method you accept — every piece of information the client needs to send money without following up to ask for it.

Set clear payment terms

Net 30 is the default in many industries, but it is not mandatory. Freelancers with strong client relationships often move to Net 14 or Net 7 without pushback. The term you set is the term you get — if you write Net 30, expect payment in 30 days at the earliest.

For new clients, request a deposit upfront — typically 30% to 50% of the project fee — before starting work. This filters out clients who were not serious about paying, and it means you are never working entirely on credit.

Important

State your payment terms explicitly on every invoice, not just in your contract. The invoice is the document the accounts payable team processes. If the terms are not on it, they may apply their company’s default schedule instead of yours.

Add a late fee clause

A late fee clause signals that you take payment timing seriously. State it clearly: invoices unpaid after the due date are subject to a 1.5% monthly late fee. Most clients will pay on time to avoid the fee — which is the entire point.

Whether you enforce the fee depends on the client relationship and how late the payment is. The clause gives you the option. Without it, you have no leverage other than stopping work.

Format matters

An invoice that looks professional signals that you run a professional operation. A Word document with misaligned columns and no invoice number signals the opposite — and some clients unconsciously prioritise payments from vendors who look established.

A clean invoice has your branding at the top, clear section headers, aligned columns, and a prominent total at the bottom. It reads in under 30 seconds. Every number is easy to find. Nothing requires interpretation.

Following up on unpaid invoices

Send a payment reminder one day before the due date — a short, friendly note that the invoice comes due tomorrow. Most late payments happen not because clients intend to pay late but because the invoice got buried in their inbox.

If payment does not arrive by the due date, follow up the next business day. Keep the tone professional and neutral — assume oversight rather than intent. If the invoice is more than two weeks overdue with no response, escalate to a phone call.

Document every follow-up. If the situation escalates to a dispute, your record of contact attempts demonstrates good faith.

Common invoice errors that delay payment

  • Wrong client name or address. Accounts payable teams sometimes reject invoices that do not match the legal entity name in their system. Confirm billing details before sending the first invoice to a new client.
  • Missing purchase order number. Larger companies require a PO number before they can process an invoice. Ask for it when the project is confirmed, not after you submit the invoice.
  • No invoice at all. Some freelancers delay sending invoices because they find the process uncomfortable. Send it the day the work is delivered, not whenever you get around to it.
Takeaway

The invoice is the mechanism that starts the payment clock. Send it the moment the work is delivered. Every day you wait is a day the payment is delayed by your own hand.
